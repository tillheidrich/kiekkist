# Presenting directions to a client

The directions are only as good as the decision they produce. A client who is shown three pages without context picks the one with the nicest photo. A client who is shown three arguments picks the one that fits their business. Your job is to make the second happen.

## What the client sees

One board page (Kiekkist board view with its share link, or a single HTML page built from `.kiekkist/board.md`) with:

1. **The brief in five lines**: audience, situation, core promise, proof, tone. The client recognises their own words and agrees to judge against them.
2. **What we looked at**: six to eight of the strongest references, grouped by cluster, each with its mechanism sentence. Show the competitor field honestly, including the anti-reference. Keep third-party screenshots inside the closed presentation; see `legal.md`.
3. **The three directions side by side**, same content, same viewport, same order:
   - name and thesis,
   - first screen at desktop and phone,
   - the two or three sections below,
   - the media probe (slide or social tile) if part of the brief,
   - three lines: what this direction says about the client, what it asks of them (photos, texts, courage), what it risks.
4. **Our recommendation** with one paragraph of reasoning against the brief. Recommend; do not hide behind neutrality. But the client decides.

## Naming for clients

Internally the labels are `safe`, `middle`, `bold`. For clients use **Mild, Medium, Hot**. It sets expectations without judging: nobody feels "unsafe" for liking Hot, and nobody feels boring for choosing Mild.

## Order of showing

Show Mild first, Hot last. Mild establishes that you understood the category; Medium shows what one deliberate shift does; Hot shows the edge. Clients judge each option against the one before, so this order makes the steps legible. The recommendation comes after all three, never before, so the client looks before being steered.

## Asking for a decision

Do not ask "which one do you like?". Ask:

- "Which of these would your best customer trust fastest, in the situation from the brief?"
- "Which one could a competitor not use?"
- "What would you have to change in your business or material to make Hot work? Is that realistic?"

Then ask for a decision, by one person. Committee votes produce averaged designs. If several people are involved, collect comments first, then let the agreed decider choose (agencies call this a supervote).

## Combinations

Combinations are allowed and often right. Write them down per axis so the system stays coherent:

> "Medium, with the type and the colour strategy of Hot."

Check a combination against the axis table: does it still hang together, or does it combine two ideas that fight (a dense index topology with an airy poster type scale)? If it fights, say so and propose the closest coherent version.

## Recording the decision

Immediately after the client decides, record it (`kk_decide` or `templates/decision.md`):

- chosen direction and any combination per axis,
- the date,
- the reason in the client's own words, quoted,
- who decided,
- what was explicitly rejected and why (from the conversation, not invented).

From this point the direction is binding. Later changes need a new decision entry with a new date; they do not happen quietly in the build.

## Remote presentation

When the client cannot meet:

- Send the share link with a short written walkthrough in the order above, or record a short screen video walking through the board.
- Ask the decision questions in writing, at most three.
- Give a date by which you need the decision; without one the project stalls.

## When the client hates all three

It happens and it is useful. Ask what exactly fails in each, using the axes: is it the structure, the type, the density, the images, the colour? Usually the brief was wrong on one link of the chain (audience, promise or tone). Correct the brief first, then build new directions. Do not tweak colours on the old ones; that is the same idea three times again.
