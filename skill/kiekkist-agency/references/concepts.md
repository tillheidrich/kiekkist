# Concept cards: where directions come from

Three directions drawn from one mental template differ only in skin. A layout scale (safe, middle, bold) on its own produces a convention, the convention plus a tweak, and the convention plus a bigger tweak. The result looks like three versions of one page, and `kk_compare` will say so.

An agency avoids this by starting each direction from a different **idea of what the thing is**. The idea usually comes from a real-world form: a document, an object or a space that already exists in the client's world or the audience's head. A tax adviser's site can be a ledger, a statute book, a field report or a waiting room. Each of those already contains a structure, a material, a voice and a typography. You borrow the logic, not the look.

## Phase 4a: write the cards (before any tokens or HTML)

Write **10 to 12 concept cards** for this client. Use the brief, the board clusters and the client's own artefacts (forms, invoices, signage, tools, vehicles, uniforms, certificates). At least four cards must come from forms outside the web (print, objects, spaces, broadcast, signage). At most two may come from the web (a dashboard, a feed, a search result).

Card format:

```
### <Name of the form>  (source: document | object | space | broadcast | signage | web)
Idea: one sentence: "The site is <form>, because <audience truth>."
Structure it gives: how the form orders information (sections, sequence, numbering, margins, index).
Material it gives: paper, enamel, glass, chalk, screen; grain, edges, light.
Voice it gives: who speaks, in which register (an examiner, a host, a mechanic, a curator).
Type it suggests: what kind of letters live on this form (and which real, self-hostable fonts fit).
Signature candidate: the one element a visitor would describe an hour later.
Risk: how this card fails (costume, gimmick, kitsch, unreadable on a phone).
```

## Catalogue of forms (starting points, not a menu)

Pick what fits the client's world. Invent new ones freely; the best card is usually one only this client could have.

| Source | Forms |
|---|---|
| Document | case file / finding (Befund), audit report, ledger, timetable, menu card, package insert, field notebook, specification sheet, contract with margin notes, school report, letter, protocol / minutes, logbook, press release, annual report, patent drawing, recipe card, invoice, certificate, index card |
| Publication | broadsheet front page, magazine feature opener, encyclopaedia entry, catalogue raisonné, atlas, phone book, mail-order catalogue, exhibition catalogue, zine, almanac, manual (Bedienungsanleitung) |
| Object | toolbox with labelled compartments, sample case, swatch book, enamel sign, ticket / boarding pass, stamp, label / packaging, instrument dial, measuring tape, map fold, slide carousel |

## Choosing three

1. Score every pair of cards on distance: does it change **structure**, **material** and **voice**? A pair that shares two of the three is too close.
2. Pick the three most distant cards that still answer the brief.
3. Assign labels afterwards: the card closest to the category's convention is `safe`, the furthest is `bold`. Labels describe risk, not quality.
4. Optional fourth, the **wildcard**: a card that breaks a brief assumption (for example drops the claim, goes dark, or speaks in the audience's voice instead of the client's). Show it only if it teaches the client something; mark it `wildcard` in the thesis.
5. Record the rejected cards in the board notes in one line each. They are material for the next round.

## Rules

- **Logic, not costume.** A "ledger" direction uses columns, running totals and ruled lines because the audience thinks in balances. It does not need fake paper texture. If the form shows only as decoration, the card failed.
- **One form per direction.** Mixing a timetable hero with a magazine body is two half-ideas.
- **The phone decides.** Before building, sketch how the form survives at 390 px. A form that only works wide is a desktop poster, not a direction.
- **Content first.** The card must hold the client's real content. If the real services do not fit the form, the card is wrong, not the content.
- **Brand-locked briefs** (fixed colours, fonts, claim, voice): the card carries the difference through structure, material, imagery and density. See "Brand-locked mode" in `directions.md`.

## Worked example: a one-person web consultancy (brand-locked)

| Card | Structure | Material | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Befund (finding) | the visitor's current site, annotated; numbered findings; recommendation | paper, red-pencil notes | the examiner |
| Fahrplan (timetable) | stops with times; where you get on, what happens at each stop | enamel board, tabular figures | the conductor |
| Werkstattwand (workshop wall) | tools on a pegboard, each labelled with what it fixes | wood, outlines of tools | the mechanic |
| Sprechstunde (consultation hour) | door sign, opening hours, questions you may bring | frosted glass, small print | the host |
| Protokoll (minutes) | a real first meeting as minutes: attendees, items, decisions, to-dos | typewritten, ruled | the minute-taker |
| Beipackzettel (package insert) | what it is for, how to use, side effects, when not to use | thin paper, folds, small dense type | the pharmacist |

Most distant three: Befund (examiner, annotation), Fahrplan (sequence, tabular), Sprechstunde (space, invitation). Werkstattwand and Protokoll were rejected as too close to Befund in voice and to Fahrplan in structure.
